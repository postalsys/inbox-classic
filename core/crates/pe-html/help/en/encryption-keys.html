Encryption keys

Make a key to sign your messages and receive encrypted mail, and keep your contacts’ keys.

Inbox Classic signs and encrypts mail with OpenPGP keys. Your own key has two halves: a public key that you give to others, and a private key that stays on your computer. Your contacts’ keys let you check their signatures and encrypt mail to them.

To make your key:

  1. Choose > , then click .
  2. Choose the account and click . The key is used for the account’s address and its other addresses.
  3. Click and choose a passphrase. Keep the file and the passphrase in different safe places.
Without a backup, your key cannot be recovered if this computer is lost or reset, and mail encrypted to it can no longer be read.

Keys stay protected without passphrases

Your private keys are protected by a key kept in the Credential Managerthe keychainthe system keyring, so you do not normally type a passphrase to use them. Passphrases protect backups and may be needed when importing. If a key is locked, first check that system storage is available and unlocked. If its protection key is missing, click and choose its backup.

Adding keys

Click to add a key from a file, such as one a contact sent you or a backup of your own. A private key protected by a passphrase asks for it once. Inbox Classic then asks whether to use an imported key of yours for your addresses.

A key copied as text, such as one pasted on a web page, can be added with . A key that arrives as an attachment can be added from the message: click the attachment and choose . Keys that come in mail are taken as public keys only; to bring back a private key, import its file here.

A contact’s key is used for their address from then on. If a different key for the same address turns up later, the current one stays in use until you choose the new one in the contact’s properties.

To share your public key, select it and click . You can also choose your account’s key in its properties, on the tab.

Finding keys

When a usable recipient key is missing, Inbox Classic asks the address’s domain first, then keys.openpgp.org if that lookup is enabled. To look for a contact’s key yourself, open the contact in the Address Book and click . The Address Book marks contacts who have a key, and those whose key you have verified.

A message signed with a key you do not have says that the key is not known. Click on its label to look the key up on keys.openpgp.org by its fingerprint; that sends the fingerprint, not an email address. If the key is found, the label then names who signed the message.

To never ask keys.openpgp.org, turn off under .

Publishing your key

So that others can find your key by your address, select it and click . Inbox Classic uploads your public key to keys.openpgp.org, and the server sends a confirmation link to each address of the key. An address is listed only after you open its link, so nobody can publish a key for an address that is not theirs.

Keys that travel with mail

Keys also travel with mail. When Autocrypt is enabled, messages from an address with a selected key carry its public key, which other apps that read it can use to encrypt to you. When you open a message that carries its sender’s key, Inbox Classic keeps that key, unverified; a different key for an address that already has one waits for you to choose. To stop sending yours, turn off on the account’s tab.

Expired, revoked and removed keys

A key that has expired, or that its owner has revoked, is shown as such in the list and is no longer used to encrypt. Signatures made with it say why they can no longer be trusted. To stop using a key, select it and click . Mail encrypted to a removed key of yours can no longer be read on this computer, so keep its backup.
